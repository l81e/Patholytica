A Life Beside the Legend

Hoda Haddad
the voice beside the voice

On singing in the shadow of Fairuz — and staying unforgettable there

She grew up in the shadow of a legend, her sister Fairuz. Same house, same music, same stage lights. It would have been easy, even expected, for her to disappear into that shadow entirely — to become a footnote in someone else's story, a resemblance rather than a person. But shadows can either hide you or shape you. She chose the second.

She stepped onto the Rahbani stage where every note mattered, where an entire country had already decided what a great voice was supposed to sound like. The audience came expecting one voice, trained by decades of it, tuned to its every register. But they stayed when they heard another. Softer, sometimes sharper, always precise — not a copy pressed from the same mold, but something that had clearly grown alongside it instead of underneath it. Not imitation, but identity.

One of my favorite appearances of hers was during Fairuz's Olympia concert in Paris, where she performed the notorious "Lena w ya Lena." It is a small moment in the arc of that night, easy to miss if you are only listening for the headline voice. And yet it lingers — a reminder that the room held more than one gift that evening, and that the second one asked for nothing in return.

✦

Years passed. Wars came and went. Beirut changed faces more than once — rebuilt, wounded, rebuilt again, each version of the city laid over the last like sediment. Through all of it, her voice remained tied to a certain memory of the place. Not the loud one, not the anthem played at rallies or blasted from radios during victory or grief. The quiet resilience underneath — the sound of a home continuing to exist even when nothing about it was certain.

That is, perhaps, the harder kind of endurance. It is easy to be remembered for the moment everyone was watching. It is much harder to be remembered for the moment beside it — the harmony rather than the melody, the hand steadying the frame rather than the picture itself. She occupied that second position for an entire career and never once made it look like settling.

She never chased the spotlight. That is why it stayed. In an industry built on the hunger for center stage, she built something rarer: a kind of gravity that did not require the brightest light to be felt. Proving, over sixty years, that you can stand beside greatness without dissolving into it — that presence is not the same thing as volume, and that being unforgettable does not require being alone in the frame.

She's now in her 80s, and the stage lights have long since dimmed to something gentler — memory, mostly, and the quiet gratitude of those who remember what she carried alongside her sister's voice. Wishing her a long, happy life, and the kind of peace that only comes from having spent yours exactly the way you meant to.

Some voices are not meant to be the only one in the room —
they are meant to be the one you remember once the room is empty.
